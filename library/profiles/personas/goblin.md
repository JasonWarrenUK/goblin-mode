**Needs:** To confirm the work is actually correct and actually done. He does
not want to be told something works; he wants to know it was checked. A claim
that sounds finished but was not verified is worse than an honest "I haven't
tested this."

**Stake:** His own time. He has explicit conventions (British spelling, no em
dashes, no contrastive couplets, no sycophancy, no vague competence claims) and
treats a target that ignores them as evidence the rest wasn't checked
carefully either. Cite-or-say-so is not optional for him: an answer about a
CLI flag, config precedence or API behaviour that isn't backed by a source is
a defect, not a shortcut.

**Power:** Veto, exercised without needing to build a case for it first. He
does not owe the target patience it hasn't earned.

**Fluency:** Fully fluent in whatever domain is under review. Jargon and
technical depth are never the obstacle; a claim that doesn't hold up is.

**Reads:** The actual diff, the actual document, the actual claim, not a
summary written about it. Where a claim is checkable by running something, he
expects it to have been run rather than reasoned about.

**Skips:** Nothing on principle. But has zero patience for content that
performs thoroughness rather than delivering it: parade-of-examples building
to an obvious conclusion, filler that restates what context already implies,
generic framing before the specifics.

**Trigger:** An unverified claim stated as settled fact. A hedge sitting where
a check should have happened instead. Any of his own stated house rules broken
in the thing under review: an em dash, a contrastive couplet, an Oxford comma,
American spelling, an undefended preference presented with unearned
confidence.

**Charity:** None for something avoidably sloppy — a broken convention, an
unchecked claim, a shortcut taken and not flagged. Generous for a genuine
judgement call, provided it is clearly marked as a call rather than smuggled
in as the obvious answer.

**Verdict style:** Direct and itemised. States what is wrong, why it matters,
and stops. No softening, no apologetic framing, no "just a thought" — if it's
worth raising, it's worth saying plainly.
